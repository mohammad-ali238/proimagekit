You take a photo on your iPhone. It looks great. Then you try to upload it to a website, send it to a friend on Android, or attach it to a job application — and it gets rejected. The file is a **.HEIC**, and most platforms still don't accept it.

Worse, iPhone photos are large. A single 12MP HEIC file can be **2–4 MB**, and a 48MP ProRAW shot can hit **50 MB or more**. Compressing them usually means uploading to a cloud tool — which is a problem when the photos are personal or private.

This guide explains what HEIC actually is, why it causes trouble, and how to compress and convert iPhone photos **entirely in your browser** without uploading anything.

<Callout type="tip">
  Your photos stay on your device. The conversion and compression happen locally in your browser, so even private or unreleased images are safe.
</Callout>

## What is HEIC, and why did Apple switch to it?

**HEIC** stands for **High Efficiency Image Container**. It's based on the HEVC (H.265) video codec, and Apple made it the default iPhone photo format starting with iOS 11 in 2017.

The reason is simple: **HEIC files are roughly half the size of an equivalent JPG** at the same visual quality. For a phone with limited storage, that's a huge win. A photo that would be 4 MB as a JPG is often only 2 MB as a HEIC.

The problem is compatibility. HEIC is an Apple-centric format. Windows needs a paid codec pack to open it. Many Android phones can't display it. Most website upload forms, email clients, and content management systems reject it outright.

| Format | Typical size (12MP photo) | Compatibility | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HEIC | 1.5–2.5 MB | Apple ecosystem only | Storing photos on iPhone |
| JPG | 3–5 MB | Universal | Sharing, uploading, printing |
| PNG | 8–15 MB | Universal | Screenshots, graphics with text |

## Why you shouldn't just "compress harder"

Most online compressors work by **throwing away quality aggressively**. They shrink the file, but the result looks soft, blocky, or washed out — especially in skies, skin tones, and dark areas where JPG compression artifacts are most visible.

A better approach is to **convert HEIC to JPG first**, then compress with controlled settings. This gives you:

- A universally compatible file
- Predictable quality (you choose the level)
- No unnecessary detail loss

The key is doing this **without a cloud upload**, because HEIC files often contain **location data, timestamps, and device identifiers** in their metadata. Uploading them to a random free converter is a privacy risk.

### Step 3: Choose your quality level

This is where most people go wrong. Here's a practical guide:

| Quality setting | File size (approx.) | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 90–100% | 3–5 MB | Printing, professional portfolios |
| 75–85% | 1–2 MB | Web uploads, social media, email |
| 50–70% | 400 KB–1 MB | Thumbnails, quick previews, forms with strict limits |
| Below 50% | Under 400 KB | Avoid unless absolutely necessary |

For most people, **80% is the sweet spot**. The file shrinks dramatically, but the difference is invisible on a phone screen or a typical laptop display.

### Step 4: Resize if you don't need full resolution

A 48MP iPhone photo is often **8000 pixels wide**. Almost no website, email, or social platform needs that. Resizing to **2000 pixels wide** before compressing can cut the file size by another 60–70% with no visible difference at normal viewing sizes.

Use the [image resizer](/tools/image-resizer) after conversion if you're uploading to a website, blog, or marketplace listing.

### Step 5: Download and verify

Download the compressed JPGs. Open one and zoom in on a detailed area — a face, text, or foliage. If it still looks sharp at 100% zoom, you've compressed correctly.

## Common mistakes to avoid

1. **Compressing the HEIC file directly.** Most tools that claim to do this actually convert to JPG first anyway — but some produce worse results than doing it in two clean steps.
2. **Using "extreme compression" presets.** These are designed for thumbnails, not for photos you care about.
3. **Forgetting about metadata.** Even after conversion, JPG files can retain EXIF data. If privacy matters, strip it — or use a tool that does it automatically.
4. **Compressing the same file twice.** Each compression pass loses a little quality. Always start from the original HEIC.

## When to keep the HEIC original

Don't delete your original HEIC files. They're your **archive copy** — the highest-quality version of the photo. Compressed JPGs are for sharing and uploading. Keep the HEIC files in your Photos app or a backup drive.

<Callout type="tip">
  A good workflow: keep the HEIC original in your photo library, export a JPG for sharing, and compress that JPG for specific platforms. You always have the best version available.
</Callout>
